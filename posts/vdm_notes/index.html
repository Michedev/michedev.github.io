Self complete guide on Variational Diffusion Models

Posted on Jul 30, 2026

Why This Post

Diffusion models are the state of the art in terms of generative models and I wanted to contribute in my own way with a self-complete article that summarizes the diffusion model training and sampling.

The Model

We define first some common terminology, then we describe the training and sampling process. Finally we define the most common ODE samplers used for generating data and we focus on the Classifier-Free guidance.

VDM Formulation

In this article we assume intuitive understand of what a generative model and what a diffusion models does (inject noise, denoise). We adopt the Variational Diffusion Model (VDM) formulation. To describe the noising process we adopt the convention

t=0  ⇒  x0  clean sample,t=1  ⇒  x1  pure noise∼N(0,I). t = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x_0\; \text{clean sample}, \qquad t = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; x_1\; \text{pure noise} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}). t=0⇒x0​clean sample,t=1⇒x1​pure noise∼N(0,I).

So ttt runs forward from data to noise during the diffusion process, and the model’s job will be to run it backward.

Training

Training a diffusion model means teaching a network parametrized by θ\thetaθ to predict the noise that was added at an arbitrary point along this schedule. Concretely, each training step does three things.

1. Sample a timestep.

t∼U[0,1]. t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]. t∼U[0,1].

2. Inject noise. Given the clean sample x0x_0x0​ , we form the noisy sample

xt=αt x0+σt ε,ε∼N(0,I), x_t = \alpha_t\, x_0 + \sigma_t\, \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), xt​=αt​x0​+σt​ε,ε∼N(0,I),

where the signal and noise coefficients αt\alpha_tαt​ and σt\sigma_tσt​ come straight from the schedule:

αt=sigmoid⁡(−γ(t)),σt=sigmoid⁡(γ(t)). \alpha_t = \sqrt{\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(-\gamma(t)\big)}, \qquad \sigma_t = \sqrt{\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(\gamma(t)\big)}. αt​=sigmoid(−γ(t))​,σt​=sigmoid(γ(t))​.

We can see how both terms are determined by γ(⋅)\gamma(\cdot)γ(⋅) , which is the log Signal-to-Noise ratio. Down below we will describe how the noise schedule γ(⋅)\gamma(\cdot)γ(⋅) can be defined.

Loss Function

With predictions in hand, the loss combines three terms:

1. Standard diffusion loss:∥ε−ε^θ(xt,t,c)∥,2. Reconstruction loss:∥ε−εθ(x0+s,Δ,c)∥,s→0+,3. Prior loss:∥ε−εθ(x1,I,c)∥. \begin{aligned} \text{1. The first term is the usual denoising objective. The last two terms anchor the endpoints of the process and, in our experience, are particularly useful when the noise schedule itself is being learned.

Once the network is trained, how do we turn pure noise back into data? That is the sampling problem. But before we get there, it’s worth pausing on the first term above — where does it actually come from, and why is it valid to leave it unweighted?

From the ELBO to the Diffusion Loss

The “standard diffusion loss” is not an arbitrary design choice. It is exactly what remains of the variational lower bound (ELBO) on log⁡pθ(x0)\log p_\theta(x_0)logpθ​(x0​) once the number of diffusion steps is taken to infinity — but only under a specific parameterization and a specific way of sampling ttt . Getting either of those wrong silently turns the training objective into something that no longer bounds the likelihood. It’s worth deriving this once end-to-end.

The catch is that “integrated uniformly over log-SNR” is doing real work here — it is not automatically satisfied just because we sample ttt uniformly.

Sampling the Timestep Correctly

The boxed identity holds only when u=γ(t)u = \gamma(t)u=γ(t) is itself distributed uniformly on [γmin⁡,γmax⁡][\gamma_{\min}, \gamma_{\max}][γmin​,γmax​] . If t∼U(0,1)t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)t∼U(0,1) , the standard 1-D change-of-variables rule tells us the induced density of uuu is

pU(u)=1∣γ′(t)∣∣t=γ−1(u). p_U(u) = \frac{1}{|\gamma'(t)|}\bigg|_{t = \gamma^{-1}(u)}. pU​(u)=∣γ′(t)∣1​​t=γ−1(u)​.

For a schedule with constant slope — the linear schedule — this density is itself constant, so sampling ttt uniformly happens to give uuu uniformly, and no correction is required. For any schedule whose slope varies with ttt (cosine, or a learned/contextual schedule that may saturate near the endpoints), t∼U(0,1)t \sim \mathcal U(0,1)t∼U(0,1) over-samples whichever noise levels correspond to the flattest regions of γ\gammaγ — wherever γ′(t)\gamma'(t)γ′(t) is small, 1/γ′(t)1/\gamma'(t)1/γ′(t) is large, so those log-SNR values get visited disproportionately often. Averaging the unweighted MSE under this mismatched sampling distribution converges to a biased quantity, not to L∞L_\inftyL∞​ .

There are two equivalent fixes, differing only in where the correction is paid.

Option A — importance-weight by γ′(t)\gamma'(t)γ′(t) . Keep t∼U(0,1)t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)t∼U(0,1) , but reweight each sample by the density ratio between the target (uniform-in- uuu ) and the induced (mismatched) distribution:

L∞=12 Et∼U(0,1)[γ′(t) ∥ε−ε^θ(xt,t)∥2]. L_\infty = \frac{1}{2}\, \mathbb{E}_{t\sim \mathcal U(0,1)}\Big[\gamma'(t)\, \big\lVert \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_\theta(x_t, t)\big\rVert^2\Big]. L∞​=21​Et∼U(0,1)​[γ′(t)​ε−ε^θ​(xt​,t)​2].

This requires the derivative of the schedule with respect to ttt . When γ\gammaγ is vector-valued — for instance, a separate log-SNR curve per output dimension — this is naturally suited to forward-mode automatic differentiation (a Jacobian-vector product): since ttt is a single scalar input mapping to many outputs, one JVP call recovers the entire per-element derivative in a single extra forward-like pass, exactly, without the truncation/round-off tuning that finite differences would require.

Option B — sample directly in log-SNR space. Draw u∼U(γmin⁡,γmax⁡)u \sim \mathcal{U}(\gamma_{\min}, \gamma_{\max})u∼U(γmin​,γmax​) first, then invert the schedule to recover the corresponding timestep,

t=γ−1(u). t = \gamma^{-1}(u). t=γ−1(u).

Because γ\gammaγ is monotonic by construction, this inversion is well-posed and can always be solved reliably by bisection on [0,1][0,1][0,1] , regardless of how nonlinear γ\gammaγ is. Since uuu is now drawn directly from the target distribution, there is no density mismatch left to correct — only the constant prefactor γmax⁡−γmin⁡\gamma_{\max}-\gamma_{\min}γmax​−γmin​ from the interval length survives, and the loss is again plain, unweighted MSE. This is the approach adopted in the original VDM paper, and it trades a derivative for a root-find.

Both estimators are unbiased for L∞L_\inftyL∞​ ; they simply relocate the computational cost. Option A is preferable when γ\gammaγ is cheap to differentiate but awkward to invert (e.g. a small MLP with no closed form); Option B is preferable when γ\gammaγ has a closed-form or cheaply-invertible structure (e.g. the cosine schedule), since it avoids computing a derivative altogether.

Sampling

We develop two types of sampling: ancestral sampling and ODE sampling. While the first relies partially on stochastic variables, the second is purely deterministic.

ODE Sampling and the VDM Coefficients

Every diffusion process has an associated forward SDE of the form

Likelihood Estimation for Diffusion Models using ODE

Continuous-time diffusion models formulate the generative process x1→x0\boldsymbol{x}_1 \to \boldsymbol{x}_0x1​→x0​ over a continuous time domain t∈[0,1]t \in [0, 1]t∈[0,1] as the path of a Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE). By mapping this SDE to an equivalent deterministic Probability-Flow Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE), we cast the generative model as a Continuous Normalizing Flow (CNF). Hutchinson Trace Estimator

Directly computing tr⁡(∇xtf~θ)\operatorname{tr}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_t} \tilde{f}_\theta)tr(∇xt​​f~​θ​) requires a full Jacobian and scales as O(d2)\mathcal{O}(d^2)O(d2) . To avoid this, we use Hutchinson’s estimator. For any matrix A∈Rd×d\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}A∈Rd×d ,

tr⁡(A)=Ev[v⊤A v], \operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{A}) = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{v}}[\boldsymbol{v}^\top \boldsymbol{A}\, \boldsymbol{v}], tr(A)=Ev​[v⊤Av],

To see unbiasedness, expand

v⊤A v=∑i=1d∑j=1dviAijvj. \boldsymbol{v}^\top \boldsymbol{A}\, \boldsymbol{v} = \sum_{i=1}^d\sum_{j=1}^d v_i A_{ij} v_j. v⊤Av=i=1∑d​j=1∑d​vi​Aij​vj​.

Taking expectation,

E[v⊤A v]=∑i=1d∑j=1dAij E[vivj]. \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{v}^\top \boldsymbol{A}\, \boldsymbol{v}] = \sum_{i=1}^d\sum_{j=1}^d A_{ij}\, \mathbb{E}[v_i v_j]. E[v⊤Av]=i=1∑d​j=1∑d​Aij​E[vi​vj​].

Because E[vivj]=δij\mathbb{E}[v_i v_j] = \delta_{ij}E[vi​vj​]=δij​ , we obtain

In diffusion likelihood estimation this is crucial: we never materialize the full Jacobian, and instead compute Jacobian-vector products, reducing practical cost from quadratic to roughly linear in dimension per probe. In practice, averaging a small number of probes (often around 555 - 101010 ) is already effective.

Choice of the Projection Vector

The projection vector v∈Rd\boldsymbol{v} \in \mathbb{R}^dv∈Rd must satisfy

E[v]=0,E[vv⊤]=I. \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{v}] = \boldsymbol{0}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{v}^\top] = \boldsymbol{I}. E[v]=0,E[vv⊤]=I.

Two common choices are:

  1. Gaussian probes: v∼N(0,I)\boldsymbol{v} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{I})v∼N(0,I) .
  2. Rademacher probes: each component is independently sampled as
vi={+1,p<0.5,−1,p≥0.5,p∼U(0,1). v_i = \begin{cases} +1, & p < 0.5,\\ -1, & p \ge 0.5, \end{cases} \qquad p \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1). vi​={+1,−1,​p<0.5,p≥0.5,​p∼U(0,1).

Both satisfy the required moment conditions and therefore produce unbiased trace estimates.

ODE Solvers

Terminology. Given a step size hhh , we want to advance from timestep tmt_mtm​ to tm+1t_{m+1}tm+1​ starting from our sample zmz_mzm​ , where f(⋅)f(\cdot)f(⋅) denotes the ODE vector field derived above. The different solvers below trade off accuracy against the number of function evaluations (NFE).

Classifier-Free Guidance

Classifier-free guidance lets us guide the generation process of a diffusion model without training a separate classifier. We denote by ccc the conditioning class.

During training.
